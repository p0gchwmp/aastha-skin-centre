What is vitiligo?

Vitiligo is a long-term condition in which areas of skin lose their natural pigment.

Pigment is produced by cells called melanocytes. In vitiligo, an autoimmune response appears to attack or destroy these cells, leading to clearly defined pale or white patches.

Vitiligo may affect:

Face

Lips and area around the mouth

Eyelids

Hands and fingers

Wrists

Elbows and knees

Underarms

Groin and genital skin

Feet

Scalp

Hair

Inside the mouth or nose

Is vitiligo contagious?

No. Vitiligo is not contagious.

It cannot spread through:

Touching

Sharing clothes or towels

Food

Kissing

Sexual contact

Living in the same household

Swimming together

Using the same toilet

Vitiligo is an autoimmune pigment disorder, not a bacterial, viral or fungal infection.

Is vitiligo caused by poor hygiene?

No.

Vitiligo is not caused by:

Dirty skin

Poor bathing habits

Eating “white foods”

Drinking milk after fish

Worms

Sharing personal items

Failure to use moisturiser

A lack of cleanliness

These myths can cause unnecessary blame and social isolation. Vitiligo develops through a combination of immune, genetic and environmental factors.

What does vitiligo look like?

Vitiligo commonly begins as one or more patches that are lighter than the surrounding skin.

Possible signs include:

Pale or completely white patches

Sharply defined borders

Gradual enlargement

Symmetrical patches on both sides

White or grey hair within a patch

Colour loss around body openings

Colour loss inside the mouth or nose

New patches after skin injury

Vitiligo may initially be less obvious in ordinary room lighting. A Wood’s lamp can make affected areas appear more clearly during examination.

Can white patches have another cause?

Yes. Not every white or pale patch is vitiligo.

Other possible causes include:

Tinea versicolor

Post-inflammatory hypopigmentation

Pityriasis alba

Chemical leukoderma

Nevus depigmentosus

Post-burn or post-injury colour loss

Steroid-related lightening

Leprosy in selected clinical settings

Other inflammatory or genetic conditions

Accurate diagnosis matters because antifungal treatment, anti-inflammatory treatment and vitiligo treatment are not interchangeable.

Is vitiligo the same as leucoderma?

Not exactly.

Leucoderma is a broad term describing white skin caused by pigment loss. Vitiligo is one specific autoimmune cause of leucoderma.

Leucoderma may also follow:

Burns

Chemical exposure

Inflammation

Trauma

Certain infections

Other skin diseases

The cause should be identified before treatment is started.

Is vitiligo the same as fungal infection?

No.

Vitiligo

Is an autoimmune pigment condition

Usually produces smooth white patches

Is not contagious

Does not improve with antifungal medicine

Fungal infection

Is caused by fungi

May produce scaling or itching

Can spread between body areas or people

Requires antifungal treatment

Tinea versicolor can produce lighter patches and is a common reason vitiligo is initially misdiagnosed.

What causes vitiligo?

The exact cause is not completely understood.

Vitiligo is believed to involve:

Autoimmune destruction of melanocytes

Genetic susceptibility

Oxidative stress

Environmental triggers

Skin injury in susceptible patients

Interactions between immune and pigment cells

A family member may also have vitiligo or another autoimmune condition, but many patients have no known family history.

Can stress cause vitiligo?

Stress alone does not explain every case.

Emotional distress, severe illness, sunburn or chemical exposure may trigger the appearance or progression of vitiligo in a genetically susceptible person. In many patients, however, no clear trigger is identified.

Patients should not be blamed for failing to “control stress.” Stress management may support wellbeing, but it does not replace medical treatment.

Can injury cause new vitiligo patches?

Yes. Some patients develop vitiligo at sites of skin injury, a reaction called the Koebner phenomenon.

Possible triggers include:

Cuts

Scratches

Burns

Tight footwear

Repeated friction

Tattoos

Piercing

Surgical wounds

Severe sunburn

Protecting the skin from unnecessary injury may help reduce this trigger in susceptible patients.

What are the main types of vitiligo?

Non-segmental vitiligo

This is the most common type.

It often:

Affects both sides of the body

Develops symmetrically

Involves hands, face or body openings

Progresses through periods of activity and stability

Segmental vitiligo

Segmental vitiligo usually:

Affects one side or region

Begins at a younger age

Spreads for a limited period

May become stable earlier

Can include white hair within the affected area

Localised or focal vitiligo

One or a few limited patches may remain confined to a small area.

Classification influences treatment planning, prognosis and whether surgery might eventually be considered.

What is active vitiligo?

Vitiligo may be considered active when:

New patches continue appearing

Existing patches enlarge

Several areas change over a short period

New patches appear after injury

Small confetti-like spots develop

Borders show signs of ongoing spread

Treatment goals during active disease include slowing or stopping progression as well as supporting repigmentation.

What is stable vitiligo?

Stable vitiligo generally means that patches have not recently enlarged and no significant new patches have appeared.

Stability is particularly important before surgical treatment is considered. Older surgical guidance commonly used approximately one year without progression as a practical benchmark, although modern assessment considers the activity of individual lesions and the overall clinical pattern.

A patient should not decide stability from photographs alone. Dermatological assessment is needed.

Can vitiligo affect hair?

Yes. Hair growing within an affected area may become white or grey.

This can involve:

Scalp hair

Eyebrows

Eyelashes

Beard

Body hair

White hair within a vitiligo patch may indicate that pigment cells associated with the hair follicle have also been affected. Such areas can be more difficult to repigment with medical treatment.

Can vitiligo affect the lips or genital area?

Yes.

Vitiligo may involve:

Lips

Around the mouth

Inside the mouth

Genital skin

Around the anus

Nipples

These areas can respond differently from facial or trunk skin. Treatment must be selected carefully because lip, genital and mucosal tissue is sensitive.

Can children develop vitiligo?

Yes. Vitiligo can begin at any age, including early childhood, and many patients notice their first patches before adulthood.

Treatment planning for a child considers:

Age

Patch location

Disease activity

Extent

School and social impact

Medicine safety

Ability to attend repeated treatment

Emotional wellbeing

Parents should avoid frightening language or blaming the child for the condition.

Is vitiligo associated with thyroid disease?

Vitiligo is associated with a higher likelihood of certain autoimmune conditions, particularly autoimmune thyroid disease.

Depending on symptoms, history and examination, Dr. Cheena Langer may consider blood tests for:

Thyroid function

Thyroid autoimmunity

Blood sugar

Vitamin or blood abnormalities

Another suspected autoimmune condition

Testing should be guided by clinical assessment rather than ordering a large standard panel for every patient.

These symptoms do not prove that another autoimmune disease is present, but they may influence the investigation plan.

How does Dr. Cheena Langer diagnose vitiligo?

A consultation may include:

Reviewing when the patches began

Checking whether patches are spreading

Examining the distribution and borders

Looking at hair within the patches

Reviewing injury or chemical exposure

Checking for scale or inflammation

Examining the skin with a Wood’s lamp

Reviewing family and autoimmune history

Assessing previous treatment

Considering blood tests or skin biopsy

Vitiligo is usually diagnosed through history, examination and Wood’s lamp assessment. A biopsy may be considered when the diagnosis remains uncertain.

What is a Wood’s lamp examination?

A Wood’s lamp is a specialised ultraviolet examination light.

It can help:

Make vitiligo patches more visible

Identify subtle or early colour loss

Clarify patch borders

Distinguish some pigment disorders

Assess facial or light-skin patches that are difficult to see

Under a Wood’s lamp, vitiligo often appears sharply defined and chalky white.

Is a skin biopsy always needed?

No.

Most typical vitiligo can be diagnosed without biopsy. A small skin sample may be recommended when:

The appearance is unusual

Inflammation or scaling is present

Another pigment disorder is suspected

The patient has reduced sensation in a patch

Treatment has produced an unexpected response

The diagnosis remains uncertain

Can vitiligo be cured permanently?

There is currently no guaranteed permanent cure.

Treatment may:

Slow or stop progression

Reduce the development of new patches

Restore pigment in selected areas

Reduce contrast between affected and unaffected skin

Support emotional wellbeing

Maintain repigmentation after improvement

Even after successful repigmentation, some colour may fade and maintenance treatment may be needed.

What are the goals of vitiligo treatment?

Treatment may aim to:

Stabilise active disease

Encourage repigmentation

Preserve existing pigment

Protect depigmented skin from sunburn

Reduce visible contrast

Treat associated autoimmune disease

Support confidence and quality of life

Not every patient chooses medical repigmentation. Observation, sunscreen and cosmetic camouflage are valid options when preferred.

Which areas respond better to treatment?

Response varies by body area.

Areas that often respond more favourably include:

Face

Neck

Trunk

Areas containing dark hair

Areas that can be more resistant include:

Fingertips

Toes

Hands

Feet

Lips

Bony prominences

Areas with white hair

The face and neck tend to respond better to light-based and topical treatment, while fingertips and toes are among the most difficult areas to repigment.

Which creams may be used for vitiligo?

Depending on age, location and extent, a treatment plan may include:

Topical corticosteroids

Tacrolimus ointment

Pimecrolimus cream

Other suitable anti-inflammatory medicines

A topical JAK inhibitor where medically and legally available

Moisturiser and sunscreen

Topical corticosteroids are generally used for limited periods because prolonged or unsuitable use can thin the skin. Calcineurin inhibitors may be considered for areas such as the face or neck and can cause temporary burning or irritation.

Are steroid creams safe for vitiligo?

They can be appropriate when prescribed at the correct strength and for the correct duration.

Unsupervised prolonged use may cause:

Skin thinning

Stretch marks

Visible blood vessels

Acne-like eruptions

Colour changes

Increased sensitivity

Eye-related risk when used improperly near the eyelids

Facial, genital and skin-fold treatment generally requires a different approach from treatment used on the trunk or limbs.

What are tacrolimus and pimecrolimus?

Tacrolimus and pimecrolimus are topical calcineurin inhibitors that reduce immune-related skin inflammation.

They may be considered for:

Face

Eyelids

Neck

Skin folds

Children in selected cases

Areas where prolonged steroid use is undesirable

Burning, warmth or irritation can occur during early use. These medicines should be prescribed according to age, area and diagnosis.

What is ruxolitinib cream?

Ruxolitinib is a topical Janus kinase, or JAK, inhibitor.

The US FDA approved ruxolitinib cream for repigmentation in non-segmental vitiligo in adults and children aged 12 years and older, with restrictions on the body area treated. It can require more than 24 weeks to produce a satisfactory response and carries medicine-specific warnings and potential side effects.

This US approval does not automatically establish availability, approval status, affordability or suitability in India. Patients should not purchase or use it without a dermatologist’s prescription and local regulatory review.

Is phototherapy used for vitiligo?

Yes. Narrowband ultraviolet B phototherapy, commonly called NB-UVB, may be considered when vitiligo is widespread, active or inadequately controlled with topical treatment.

Phototherapy:

Uses a medically controlled ultraviolet dose

Requires repeated sessions

Works gradually

May be combined with topical medicine

Often responds better on the face and neck

Can cause temporary redness, dryness or itching

Aastha Skin Centre’s verified technology inventory does not currently list an NB-UVB phototherapy unit. Patients who require this treatment may need referral or coordinated care with an appropriate phototherapy centre.

Can an excimer laser treat vitiligo?

A targeted ultraviolet laser or lamp may be used for selected localised patches.

Compared with a full-body phototherapy cabinet, targeted treatment exposes a smaller skin area. It still requires repeated sessions and works less predictably on the lips, fingertips, hands and feet.

Excimer treatment is not listed in Aastha Skin Centre’s verified device inventory, so it should not be advertised as an in-clinic service unless the equipment and active location are separately confirmed.

Are oral medicines used when vitiligo is spreading?

Selected oral anti-inflammatory medicines may be considered when vitiligo is progressing rapidly.

The goal is usually to slow immune activity and reduce the appearance of new patches rather than produce immediate repigmentation. Systemic corticosteroids and other immune-modifying medicines can cause significant side effects, so they require individual assessment and monitoring.

Patients should not take steroid tablets or repeat an older prescription independently.

Can vitiligo be treated surgically?

Surgical treatment may be considered for selected stable, treatment-resistant vitiligo.

Options used in specialist centres include:

Mini-punch grafting

Suction-blister grafting

Split-thickness skin grafting

Melanocyte or epidermal cell transplantation

Follicular grafting in selected cases

Surgery is generally avoided when patches are actively spreading or when the patient forms keloids or abnormal scars.

Vitiligo surgery is not listed as a confirmed service in the clinic’s current branch-wise treatment matrix. Patients who may benefit should be assessed and referred to an appropriate surgical centre when needed.

Can cosmetic camouflage help?

Yes.

Options may include:

Skin camouflage makeup

Waterproof corrective products

Self-tanning products

Skin dyes

Colour-correcting cosmetics

Camouflage provides immediate visual blending without changing the underlying disease. It may be used while medical treatment is working or as a preferred alternative to repigmentation treatment.

Products should be patch-tested when the skin is sensitive.

Should people with vitiligo use sunscreen?

Yes. Depigmented skin contains less protective melanin and can burn more easily.

Helpful sun-protection measures include:

Broad-spectrum sunscreen

SPF 30 or higher

Protective clothing

Hats and shade

Reapplication during outdoor activity

Avoiding deliberate tanning

Avoiding sunbeds

Sun protection also reduces tanning of unaffected skin, which can make the contrast with white patches more noticeable.

Can sun exposure treat vitiligo naturally?

Uncontrolled sunlight is not a substitute for supervised phototherapy.

Excessive exposure can cause:

Sunburn

Skin injury

Koebner-related new patches

Premature ageing

Uneven tanning

Greater contrast between affected and unaffected skin

Medical phototherapy uses a measured wavelength and controlled dose. Sunbathing cannot reproduce that precision.

Do diet or supplements cure vitiligo?

No specific diet, vitamin, herb or detox plan has been proven to permanently cure vitiligo.

Testing or supplementation may be appropriate when a genuine nutritional deficiency is suspected or confirmed. Patients should be cautious about unregulated products claiming to:

Permanently restore all pigment

Purify the blood

Reverse autoimmunity

Cure vitiligo through food restriction

Produce guaranteed colour within days

A balanced diet supports general health but should not replace dermatological treatment.

Should milk, fish or sour food be avoided?

There is no established medical basis for the common belief that eating fish with milk causes vitiligo.

Patients do not routinely need to avoid:

Milk

Curd

Fish

Citrus fruit

Tomatoes

White-coloured foods

Salt

Ordinary home-cooked meals

Unnecessary dietary restrictions can lead to poor nutrition and add stress without changing the disease.

Can tattoos hide vitiligo?

Ordinary decorative tattooing or permanent makeup requires caution.

Skin injury may trigger new vitiligo through the Koebner phenomenon. Tattoo colour may also look uneven if the disease progresses, and pigment can complicate future examination or treatment.

Medical micropigmentation may be discussed for carefully selected stable areas, but it is not suitable for active vitiligo or every body site.

Can vitiligo return after repigmentation?

Yes.

Repigmented areas may lose colour again because vitiligo remains an autoimmune condition. Relapse can occur after:

Treatment is stopped

Sunburn

Skin injury

Disease reactivation

Major stress or illness

Inadequate maintenance

Maintenance treatment may be advised after repigmentation, particularly in areas that have previously relapsed.

How long does vitiligo treatment take?

Vitiligo treatment usually works slowly.

The timeframe depends on:

Type of vitiligo

Disease activity

Body area

Age of the patch

Presence of white hair

Treatment selected

Adherence

Individual response

Meaningful repigmentation often requires several months, and some areas may not respond despite correct treatment.

A treatment should not be judged after only a few days.

How does vitiligo affect emotional wellbeing?

Vitiligo may affect:

Confidence

School or work

Relationships

Clothing choices

Social interaction

Photography

Anxiety

Mood

Fear of rejection

The emotional burden can be significant even when the physical area involved is small. Current consensus recognises that body-surface area alone does not fully capture vitiligo severity or its impact.

Counselling, peer support or camouflage may be valuable alongside medical treatment.

How can parents support a child with vitiligo?

Parents can help by:

Explaining that vitiligo is not contagious

Avoiding blame or frightening predictions

Correcting myths within the family

Informing teachers when bullying occurs

Allowing the child to participate in treatment decisions

Supporting sunscreen use

Offering camouflage only when the child wants it

Seeking counselling when confidence or mood is affected

Camouflage can improve confidence for some children, but it should remain a personal choice rather than a message that their skin must be hidden.

When should vitiligo be reassessed?

Arrange review when:

New patches continue appearing

Existing patches enlarge

Hair within patches turns white

Treatment causes burning or thinning

The face or genital area is affected

A child develops new patches

Symptoms suggest thyroid disease

Emotional distress is increasing

Repigmentation begins fading

Early reassessment can distinguish active progression from stable disease and allow treatment to be adjusted.

Frequently asked questions

Is vitiligo contagious?

No. Vitiligo is an autoimmune pigment condition and cannot spread through touching, food, towels, sexual contact or living together.

Can vitiligo be cured permanently?

There is currently no guaranteed permanent cure. Treatment may stop progression and restore colour, but repigmentation can fade and maintenance may be required.

Is every white patch vitiligo?

No. Fungal infection, post-inflammatory hypopigmentation and several other conditions can produce pale or white patches. Examination and a Wood’s lamp may help confirm the diagnosis.

Is vitiligo associated with thyroid disease?

Vitiligo is associated with an increased likelihood of autoimmune thyroid disease. Blood tests may be considered according to symptoms, medical history and examination.

Which areas respond best to vitiligo treatment?

The face and neck often respond better, while the fingertips, toes, lips, hands and feet can be more resistant. Results differ between patients and treatment types.

Can children receive vitiligo treatment?

Yes. Children may receive age-appropriate topical treatment, sun protection and other care according to the type, location and activity of vitiligo.

Does diet cure vitiligo?

No diet, food combination or supplement has been proven to permanently cure vitiligo. Unnecessary food restrictions should not replace medical treatment.
