What is psoriasis?

Psoriasis is a long-lasting immune-mediated inflammatory disease.

In healthy skin, new cells normally develop and move towards the surface gradually. In psoriasis, this process becomes unusually rapid. Skin cells accumulate before older cells can shed normally, producing thickened patches and scale.

Psoriasis can affect:

Skin

Scalp

Fingernails and toenails

Palms and soles

Skin folds

Genital skin

Joints in people who develop psoriatic arthritis

Symptoms often follow a cycle, with periods of improvement followed by unpredictable flare-ups.

Is psoriasis contagious?

No. Psoriasis is not contagious.

It cannot spread through:

Touching

Hugging

Sharing towels

Swimming together

Sexual contact

Sharing food

Living in the same household

Psoriasis is related to immune-system activity, genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers. It is not caused by an infection that can pass from one person to another.

Is psoriasis caused by poor hygiene?

No.

Psoriasis is not caused by:

Dirty skin

Infrequent bathing

Poor personal hygiene

Eating a particular food

A fungal infection

Failure to exfoliate

Contact with another patient

Aggressive scrubbing can irritate the skin and may worsen psoriasis rather than remove it.

What does psoriasis look like?

The appearance varies according to the type, body area and natural skin tone.

Common signs include:

Raised or thickened patches

Dry scale

Itching

Burning

Soreness

Cracking

Bleeding after scratching

Flaking from the scalp

Changes in the nails

Plaque psoriasis frequently affects the elbows, knees, scalp and lower back, although it can appear anywhere.

How does psoriasis appear on Indian skin?

On lighter skin, psoriasis often looks pink or red with silvery-white scale.

On medium-to-deep skin tones, it may look:

Violet

Purple-brown

Dark brown

Greyish

Less visibly red

More thickly scaled

After a flare clears, Indian skin may retain temporary darker or lighter patches. These colour changes are not necessarily scars and may take months to fade.

This difference in appearance can sometimes cause psoriasis to be mistaken for fungal infection, eczema or pigmentation.

What are the main types of psoriasis?

Plaque psoriasis

This is the most common form. It produces raised plaques with scale, frequently on the scalp, elbows, knees or lower back.

Guttate psoriasis

Guttate psoriasis produces many small scaly spots, often across the trunk, arms or legs. It can begin after an infection such as streptococcal throat infection and is seen commonly in children and young adults.

Inverse psoriasis

Inverse or flexural psoriasis affects skin folds such as:

Underarms

Groin

Beneath the breasts

Buttock crease

It may appear smooth, shiny, sore and inflamed, with less obvious scale because of moisture and friction.

Pustular psoriasis

Pustular psoriasis causes pus-filled bumps on inflamed skin. Localised forms may affect the palms and soles. Widespread pustular psoriasis accompanied by fever or chills requires urgent medical care.

Erythrodermic psoriasis

Erythrodermic psoriasis causes extensive inflammation and shedding over a large part of the body. It may disturb body temperature and fluid balance and can become life-threatening.

What is scalp psoriasis?

Scalp psoriasis can affect a small area or cover most of the scalp.

It may cause:

Thick scale

Dandruff-like flaking

Itching

Burning

Cracking

Bleeding after scratching

Patches extending behind the ears or beyond the hairline

Temporary hair shedding

Scalp psoriasis can resemble ordinary dandruff or seborrhoeic dermatitis. Diagnosis is important because treatment products and strengths may differ.

Hair usually regrows when inflammation settles, although forceful scratching or pulling away adherent scale can increase breakage and shedding.

What is nail psoriasis?

Psoriasis may affect one or more fingernails or toenails.

Possible signs include:

Tiny pits

Yellow, white or brown discolouration

Crumbling

Thickening

Separation from the nail bed

Debris beneath the nail

Pain or tenderness

Nail psoriasis may occur with or without obvious skin plaques. Nail changes can also be associated with psoriatic arthritis, so they should be mentioned during consultation.

Fungal nail infection can look similar and may need testing before treatment.

Can psoriasis affect the face or genital area?

Yes.

Psoriasis can affect:

Eyelids

Hairline

Behind the ears

Around the nose

Genital skin

Groin folds

Buttock crease

These areas have thinner or more sensitive skin, so medication prescribed for an elbow or knee may be unsuitable for the face or genital region. A separate treatment plan may be needed.

Genital psoriasis is not sexually transmitted and cannot be passed to a partner.

What causes psoriasis?

Psoriasis appears to result from an interaction between:

Genetic susceptibility

Immune-system activity

Environmental triggers

Having a relative with psoriasis can increase risk, but not everyone with a family history develops the condition. A trigger may cause psoriasis to appear or flare in a genetically susceptible person.

What can trigger a psoriasis flare?

Triggers differ between patients and may include:

Emotional stress

Skin injury

Cuts or scratches

Sunburn

Throat or other infections

Cold, dry weather

Smoking

Heavy alcohol use

Certain medicines

Abrupt changes to some systemic treatments

Patients should not stop a prescribed medicine independently. Tell the prescribing doctor when a suspected medicine appears to worsen psoriasis.

Can an injury cause a new psoriasis patch?

Yes. Psoriasis can sometimes appear in an area of injured skin.

Possible triggers include:

Scratching

Cuts

Burns

Tattoos

Insect bites

Surgical wounds

Repeated friction

Severe sunburn

Avoiding preventable skin injury and treating itching can help reduce this type of flare.

Is psoriasis the same as fungal infection?

No.

Psoriasis

Chronic inflammatory condition

Not contagious

May affect scalp, nails and joints

Often recurs in similar areas

Requires anti-inflammatory or immune-directed treatment

Fungal infection

Caused by fungi

Can spread between people or body areas

Often requires antifungal medicine

Both can cause scaling and itching. Using a steroid-containing mixed cream on an undiagnosed fungal infection can change its appearance and allow it to spread.

Is psoriasis the same as eczema?

No, although the conditions can occasionally look similar.

Eczema commonly produces intense itching, dryness and inflamed patches. Psoriasis more often produces sharply defined, thickened plaques with scale, but these textbook differences are not present in every patient.

Both conditions can affect:

Scalp

Face

Hands

Skin folds

Children

Adults

A dermatologist may occasionally recommend a skin biopsy when the diagnosis remains uncertain.

Assessment may include:

Examining the skin and scalp

Checking fingernails and toenails

Reviewing itching, pain and flare duration

Asking about family history

Reviewing recent infections or stress

Reviewing medicines

Asking about joint pain or stiffness

Assessing the extent and location of plaques

Psoriasis is often diagnosed clinically. A small skin sample may be examined when another condition needs to be excluded.

How is psoriasis severity assessed?

Severity is not determined only by the total area involved.

Assessment may consider:

Percentage of body surface affected

Plaque redness, thickness and scale

Scalp, nail, palm or sole involvement

Genital or facial involvement

Itching and pain

Cracking and bleeding

Effect on work or school

Effect on sleep

Psychological or social impact

Response to previous treatment

Even a small area can be severe when it affects the hands, feet, face, genitals or nails. NICE recommends considering both physical severity and the impact on psychological and social wellbeing.

Can psoriasis be cured permanently?

There is currently no permanent cure, but treatment can provide substantial control.

Patients may experience:

Fewer plaques

Less itching

Less scale

Clear or nearly clear skin

Longer periods between flares

Better sleep and daily comfort

Psoriasis commonly requires a long-term strategy. Treatment may change over time according to disease activity, life stage, other illnesses and response.

What are the goals of psoriasis treatment?

Treatment aims to:

Reduce inflammation

Relieve itching, burning and pain

Remove or soften scale

Clear or reduce plaques

Treat scalp and nail disease

Improve sleep and daily functioning

Reduce psychological impact

Identify joint symptoms early

Maintain control with the least burdensome suitable plan

No single treatment works for every patient. Dermatologists may use one treatment or combine several approaches.

Which topical treatments may be used?

Mild or limited psoriasis is often managed with medicine applied directly to the skin.

Depending on the location and patient, a treatment plan may include:

Topical corticosteroids

Vitamin D–related medicines

Scale-softening treatments

Selected retinoid preparations

Coal-tar preparations

Calcineurin inhibitors for sensitive areas

Newer non-steroid prescription preparations

Moisturisers and emollients

The strength and duration must be matched to the body area. Face, groin and skin-fold treatment generally differs from treatment used on thick plaques over elbows or knees.

Are steroid creams safe for psoriasis?

Topical corticosteroids are widely used and can reduce inflammation, itching and plaque thickness.

Safe use depends on:

Correct strength

Correct body area

Correct amount

Appropriate duration

Planned breaks or maintenance

Follow-up when treatment is prolonged

Strong steroids used excessively can cause thinning, stretch marks, visible blood vessels, acne-like eruptions or rebound problems. Patients should not repeatedly apply an old prescription without review.

Steroid-antifungal-antibiotic combination creams should not be used as a general solution for every itchy rash.

Does moisturiser help psoriasis?

Yes. Moisturiser does not cure psoriasis, but it can support almost every treatment plan.

Regular moisturising may help:

Reduce dryness

Soften scale

Ease cracking

Reduce discomfort

Support the skin barrier

Reduce irritation caused by friction

AAD guidance notes that moisturisers can benefit patients across different levels of psoriasis severity.

Fragrance-free ointments or thick creams are often better tolerated than highly perfumed cosmetic lotions.

What treatment is used for scalp psoriasis?

Depending on severity, scalp treatment may include:

Medicated shampoo

Scale-softening treatment

Topical solution, lotion, gel or foam

Corticosteroid preparation

Vitamin D–related treatment

Phototherapy in selected cases

Systemic treatment when scalp disease is extensive

Treatment needs to reach the scalp rather than remain only on the hair. Patients should avoid forcefully pulling off scale, which can injure the scalp and worsen hair shedding.

What treatment is used for nail psoriasis?

Nail psoriasis can be slow to improve because nails grow gradually.

Treatment may involve:

Medicine applied around or beneath the nail

Topical corticosteroid

Vitamin D–related treatment

Selected retinoid treatment

Injection around the nail in suitable cases

Systemic medicine for extensive skin and nail disease

Assessment for accompanying fungal infection

Treatment may need to continue for several months before meaningful change becomes visible.

What is phototherapy for psoriasis?

Phototherapy exposes affected skin to a medically controlled type and dose of ultraviolet light.

Narrowband UVB may be considered for selected patients with:

Widespread plaque psoriasis

Guttate psoriasis

Disease not adequately controlled with topical treatment

A need to avoid or delay certain systemic medicines

Phototherapy can slow rapid skin-cell growth and reduce inflammation, but it usually requires repeated supervised sessions over several weeks.

Aastha Skin Centre does not currently list a psoriasis phototherapy unit in its verified technology inventory. Patients who require narrowband UVB may need referral or coordination with an appropriate treatment centre.

Commercial tanning beds are not a substitute for prescribed medical phototherapy.

When are tablets or injections considered?

Systemic treatment may be considered when psoriasis:

Is moderate or severe

Covers an extensive area

Seriously affects daily life

Affects difficult sites

Has not responded adequately to topical treatment

Is associated with psoriatic arthritis

Requires frequent intensive treatment

Cannot be managed safely with topical treatment alone

Options may include non-biologic medicines, targeted oral medicines and biologic injections. Treatment choice depends on medical history, pregnancy plans, infection risk, other medicines, laboratory results and patient preference.

What are biologic medicines?

Biologics are medicines that target selected parts of the immune pathway involved in psoriasis.

They may be considered for moderate-to-severe psoriasis or psoriatic arthritis when clinically appropriate. They can produce substantial improvement for selected patients but also require evaluation for infection risk and ongoing medical monitoring.

Before starting systemic or biologic treatment, the dermatologist may request tests based on the medicine being considered. These can include blood counts, liver and kidney tests, tuberculosis screening or tests for selected infections.

Why are blood tests needed with some treatments?

Some psoriasis medicines can affect:

Liver

Kidneys

Blood-cell counts

Blood pressure

Infection risk

Pregnancy

Tests help determine whether a medicine is suitable and whether it remains safe during treatment.

For example, methotrexate requires medical review and laboratory monitoring and must be taken exactly as prescribed.

Patients should bring a complete list of prescription medicines, supplements and previous psoriasis treatments to the consultation.

Some topical treatments and medically supervised UVB phototherapy may be considered, while medicines such as methotrexate, acitretin and topical tazarotene are unsuitable during pregnancy.

Can children develop psoriasis?

Yes. Psoriasis can begin during childhood or adolescence.

In children it may resemble:

Eczema

Diaper rash

Cradle cap

Fungal infection

A widespread rash after throat infection

Treatment must consider the child’s age, weight, body area, disease impact and long-term safety.

What is psoriatic arthritis?

Psoriatic arthritis is an inflammatory condition affecting joints and areas where tendons or ligaments attach to bone.

Warning signs include:

Swollen or tender joints

Morning stiffness

Stiffness after prolonged sitting

A swollen entire finger or toe

Heel pain

Lower-back pain

Nail pitting

Nail separation

Early diagnosis matters because untreated inflammatory arthritis can cause progressive joint damage. NICE recommends regular assessment for psoriatic arthritis in people with psoriasis.

Patients with suspicious joint symptoms may require rheumatology assessment.

Can psoriasis affect health beyond the skin?

Yes. Psoriasis is associated with several health conditions, including:

Psoriatic arthritis

High blood pressure

Diabetes

Abnormal cholesterol

Cardiovascular disease

Inflammatory bowel disease

Anxiety and depression

This does not mean every patient will develop these conditions. Risk assessment depends on severity, age, family history, smoking, weight and other health factors.

Adults with severe psoriasis may need cardiovascular-risk assessment alongside their skin treatment.

Can stress worsen psoriasis?

Stress is a recognised trigger for many patients and psoriasis itself can create further emotional distress.

Visible plaques, itching, flaking or genital involvement may affect:

Confidence

Relationships

Sleep

Work

School

Social activity

Mood

Treating both the skin and its psychological impact is part of appropriate psoriasis care. NICE recommends assessing the physical, psychological and social effect of the condition.

Patients experiencing persistent anxiety, depression or thoughts of self-harm should seek professional mental-health support promptly.

Do diet changes cure psoriasis?

No particular food or “detox” plan has been proven to cure psoriasis.

A balanced lifestyle can still support general health, especially because psoriasis may occur alongside cardiovascular and metabolic risk factors.

Helpful goals may include:

Maintaining a healthy weight

Avoiding smoking

Limiting excessive alcohol

Staying physically active

Managing diabetes or blood pressure

Eating a balanced diet

Following prescribed treatment

Lifestyle changes should support medical care rather than replace it.

Patients should be cautious about unregulated supplements claiming to permanently cleanse psoriasis from the body.

What daily skin care can help?

Patients may be advised to:

Moisturise regularly

Use lukewarm rather than very hot water

Choose a gentle cleanser

Avoid vigorous scrubbing

Avoid picking scale

Keep nails short

Protect the skin from cuts and sunburn

Use prescribed medicine consistently

Identify personal triggers

Attend follow-up appointments

Scratching and skin injury can worsen plaques, while appropriate treatment and moisturising can help reduce itching and cracking.

When does psoriasis require urgent care?

Seek urgent hospital or same-day specialist assessment for:

Widespread pus-filled blisters

Fever or chills with pustules

Skin becoming extensively red and peeling

Severe weakness

Dehydration

Rapidly worsening widespread inflammation

Difficulty maintaining body temperature

Signs of serious infection

Severe medicine reaction

Generalised pustular psoriasis and erythrodermic psoriasis can become life-threatening.

Frequently asked questions

Is psoriasis contagious?

No. Psoriasis is an immune-mediated inflammatory condition and cannot spread through touch, towels, swimming, food or sexual contact.

Can psoriasis be cured permanently?

There is currently no permanent cure, but suitable treatment can provide clear or nearly clear skin, reduce symptoms and keep the condition controlled.

What causes psoriasis?

Psoriasis develops through a combination of genetic susceptibility, immune-system activity and environmental triggers such as stress, infection, skin injury or certain medicines.

Is scalp psoriasis the same as dandruff?

No. Both can cause flaking, but scalp psoriasis can form thicker, inflamed plaques and may extend beyond the hairline. Examination helps distinguish them.

Can psoriasis affect the joints?

Yes. Psoriatic arthritis can cause swollen joints, morning stiffness, heel pain, back pain or swelling of an entire finger or toe. Early assessment is important.

Can steroid creams treat psoriasis?

Topical corticosteroids are commonly used, but the correct strength, body area and duration matter. Strong or prolonged unsupervised use can cause side effects.
