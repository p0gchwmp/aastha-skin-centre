What are dark circles?

Dark circles are darker, bluish, purple, brown or shadowed areas beneath or around the eyes.

The medical terms may include:

Periorbital hyperpigmentation

Periocular pigmentation

Infraorbital dark circles

Periorbital melanosis

Under-eye discolouration

They are usually a cosmetic concern rather than a dangerous disease, but the appearance may be caused by several different skin, vascular and structural factors.

What are the main types of dark circles?

Dark circles can be broadly classified as:

Pigment-related dark circles

Brown or grey pigmentation is present within the skin.

Vascular dark circles

Blue, violet or reddish tones appear because blood vessels are visible through thin under-eye skin.

Structural dark circles

A hollow tear trough, eye bag or facial contour creates a shadow.

Post-inflammatory dark circles

Pigmentation follows eczema, contact dermatitis, rubbing or another inflammatory rash.

Mixed dark circles

More than one factor is present, such as pigment combined with tear-trough hollowing.

This classification matters because pigment creams cannot fill a hollow, while filler does not remove genuine epidermal pigmentation.

What causes brown under-eye circles?

Brown under-eye circles may result from:

Genetic or constitutional pigmentation

Sun exposure

Atopic dermatitis

Allergic contact dermatitis

Repeated rubbing

Previous inflammation

Pigmentation extending from nearby facial skin

Age-related skin changes

Certain medicines or cosmetic products

Brown colour that remains in different lighting usually suggests a pigment component, although many patients have a mixed pattern.

What causes blue or purple under-eye circles?

Blue, purple or reddish colour may become visible when:

Under-eye skin is naturally thin

Superficial blood vessels are prominent

Skin becomes thinner with age

There is temporary venous congestion

Swelling makes vascular colour more obvious

Pigmentation and visible vessels occur together

Treatments aimed only at melanin may produce limited improvement when the main problem is vascular visibility.

What is tear-trough shadowing?

The tear trough is the hollow extending from the inner corner of the lower eyelid towards the upper cheek.

Inherited facial anatomy, age-related volume loss, ligament changes and descent of nearby tissues can deepen this hollow. Light falling across it creates a shadow that looks like a dark circle even when the skin itself is not heavily pigmented.

Are under-eye bags the same as dark circles?

No.

Under-eye bags are areas of puffiness or protruding tissue beneath the lower eyelids. They can create a shadow below the swelling, making the area appear dark.

A patient can have:

Dark circles without bags

Bags without pigmentation

Tear-trough hollowing with bags

Pigment, hollowing and puffiness together

Large or persistent bags may require an oculoplastic or plastic-surgical opinion rather than pigmentation treatment alone.

Are dark circles caused only by lack of sleep?

No.

Poor sleep may make existing dark circles, puffiness or a tired appearance more noticeable, but persistent under-eye darkness is often influenced by genetics, pigmentation, anatomy, skin thickness or inflammation.

Someone who sleeps well can still have hereditary pigment or a deep tear trough. Likewise, improving sleep may help overall appearance without eliminating the underlying cause.

Are dark circles hereditary?

Yes.

Some people naturally have:

More pigment around the eyes

Thinner lower-eyelid skin

More visible vessels

A deeper tear trough

Facial bone structure that creates shadowing

Hereditary dark circles may begin during childhood or adolescence and can become more noticeable with age. Treatment may reduce their appearance, but permanent complete removal should not be promised.

Can allergies cause dark circles?

Allergic conditions can contribute indirectly through:

Eyelid inflammation

Watering

Puffiness

Nasal congestion

Frequent rubbing

Post-inflammatory pigmentation

Treating associated allergy or dermatitis and stopping repeated rubbing may be an important part of the plan. Dark circles should not automatically be labelled an allergy without compatible symptoms and examination.

Can rubbing the eyes make dark circles worse?

Yes.

Repeated rubbing can:

Irritate thin eyelid skin

Worsen eczema

Trigger contact dermatitis

Produce post-inflammatory pigmentation

Make swelling and blood vessels more visible

Cause additional fine lines over time

The reason for rubbing—such as allergy, dry eyes, eczema or a cosmetic reaction—should be addressed rather than simply telling the patient to stop.

Can eczema cause under-eye pigmentation?

Yes.

Eczema can affect the eyelids and surrounding skin, causing itching, dryness, scaling and swelling. Repeated inflammation and scratching may leave persistent brown, grey or purple pigmentation after the active eczema improves.

Active eczema should be controlled before a chemical peel, laser or strong pigmentation cream is considered.

Eyelash adhesive

A product used on the hair, nails or hands can reach the eyelids indirectly through touching.

Can ageing cause dark circles?

Yes.

Ageing may lead to:

Thinner skin

Reduced collagen

Greater vessel visibility

Deepening of the tear trough

Mid-face volume loss

Lower-eyelid fat prominence

Skin laxity

Fine lines and wrinkles

These changes can create both true discolouration and stronger shadows. Structural ageing generally requires a different approach from pigment-based dark circles.

Can sun exposure worsen under-eye pigmentation?

Yes.

Ultraviolet exposure can deepen melanin-related pigmentation and increase contrast between the under-eye area and surrounding skin. Sun protection is therefore an important part of pigment-based treatment and may also reduce post-inflammatory darkening after procedures.

Do dark circles mean someone has anaemia?

Not necessarily.

Dark circles alone cannot diagnose anaemia. Blood testing may be considered when a patient also reports symptoms such as:

Unusual fatigue

Weakness

Breathlessness

Paleness

Dizziness

Heavy menstrual bleeding

Nutritional concerns

Routine iron supplementation should not be started solely because under-eye circles are present.

Can thyroid or kidney disease cause under-eye changes?

Some medical conditions can cause puffiness or facial changes, but ordinary dark circles alone are not enough to diagnose thyroid, kidney or another systemic disease.

Additional medical assessment may be appropriate when under-eye change occurs with:

Generalised facial swelling

Leg swelling

Weight change

Heat or cold intolerance

Palpitations

Severe fatigue

Changes in urination

Other systemic symptoms

Testing should be guided by history and examination rather than ordered as an automatic dark-circle package.

How do dark circles appear on Indian skin?

On Indian and deeper skin tones, dark circles may appear:

Light brown

Deep brown

Grey-brown

Purple-brown

Blue-grey

Almost black under certain lighting

Post-inflammatory pigmentation from rubbing, eczema and cosmetic reactions may be especially noticeable. Procedures that cause excessive irritation can worsen pigmentation, so treatment intensity must be selected carefully.

Can a stretch test help identify the cause?

During assessment, gently stretching or repositioning the lower-eyelid skin may help distinguish:

True pigment

Visible vascular colour

Shadowing caused by a hollow

A mixed pattern

If darkness improves substantially when the skin or cheek is repositioned, structural shadowing may be contributing. Clinical examination remains more useful than relying on a selfie or one lighting condition.

Are blood tests always required?

No.

Blood tests may be considered when the history suggests:

Anaemia

Nutritional deficiency

Thyroid disease

Systemic swelling

Another medical condition

Most hereditary, pigmentary or tear-trough dark circles do not require an extensive investigation package.

Can dark circles be removed permanently?

Complete permanent removal cannot be guaranteed.

Treatment may:

Lighten genuine pigmentation

Reduce inflammation

Improve surface texture

Reduce vascular visibility

Soften tear-trough shadowing

Improve under-eye contour

Make the area appear less tired

The result depends on the cause. Hereditary anatomy, natural skin thickness and ageing continue over time, so maintenance may be required.

What is the first step in treatment?

The first step is treating the dominant cause.

This may mean:

Controlling eyelid eczema

Stopping an allergenic product

Reducing rubbing

Improving sun protection

Treating pigment carefully

Addressing tear-trough hollowing

Referring persistent eye bags for surgical assessment

Investigating relevant medical symptoms

Starting several strong treatments simultaneously can make it difficult to identify what helped or caused irritation.

Which skincare measures may help?

A basic under-eye routine may include:

Gentle fragrance-free cleansing

A suitable moisturiser

Broad-spectrum sunscreen

Avoiding repeated rubbing

Removing eye makeup gently

Treating active dermatitis

Avoiding harsh scrubs and strong home peels

Using prescribed products only in the advised area

The eyelid region is thin and easily irritated, so products suitable for the cheeks may be too strong for use close to the eye.

Which topical medicines may be used?

Depending on the cause and skin sensitivity, a dermatologist may consider:

A topical retinoid

Azelaic acid

Vitamin C

A carefully supervised depigmenting agent

A barrier-repair moisturiser

Anti-inflammatory treatment for eczema

Another pigment-modulating ingredient

Systematic reviews suggest that topical creams and serums may help pigment-based dark circles, but the available evidence is limited and results vary.

Is hydroquinone used under the eyes?

Hydroquinone may be considered for selected pigment-based cases, but the eyelid area requires cautious use.

Incorrect strength, prolonged use or application too close to the eye may cause:

Irritation

Dryness

Contact dermatitis

Uneven lightening

Rebound pigmentation

Rare long-term complications

It should not be used indefinitely or purchased in strong unregulated combinations without medical supervision.

Can retinoids improve dark circles?

A carefully selected topical retinoid may help with:

Mild pigmentation

Fine lines

Surface texture

Photoageing

Skin remodelling

Retinoids can also cause burning, peeling and dermatitis, which may worsen under-eye pigmentation if the treatment is too strong. Pregnancy or pregnancy planning must be disclosed before use.

Can caffeine eye creams remove dark circles?

Caffeine-containing products may temporarily reduce the appearance of puffiness or vascular congestion in some patients, but they do not permanently correct:

Deep pigmentation

A tear-trough hollow

Eye bags

Significant skin laxity

Hereditary anatomy

An eye cream should be viewed as supportive skincare rather than a guaranteed cure.

Can chemical peels treat under-eye pigmentation?

A carefully selected superficial chemical peel may help selected pigment-based dark circles.

Possible benefits include:

Gradual lightening

Smoother texture

Improved surface brightness

Better response when combined with suitable home care

Potential risks include burning, irritation, crusting, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and uneven colour. Evidence supports chemical peels mainly for pigment-based dark circles, but high-quality comparative research remains limited.

Chemical peels are available at both Aastha Skin Centre branches. The product, strength and distance from the eyelid margin must be selected after examination.

Can laser treatment improve dark circles?

Laser or light-based treatment may help selected pigmentary, vascular or textural components.

However:

Different devices target different causes

Not every wavelength is suitable for every skin tone

The eyelid area requires specialised eye protection

Several sessions may be needed

Pigmentation can temporarily worsen

Structural hollowing will not be corrected by pigment laser alone

Systematic reviews report that light devices can improve selected periocular pigmentation, but results vary and better-standardised clinical trials are still needed.

Can IPL photofacial help?

IPL may be considered for selected superficial pigment or vascular components, depending on skin tone and the exact location of discolouration.

A 2024 systematic review reported favourable outcomes from IPL in some pigmented dark-circle studies, particularly when combined with depigmenting treatment. The review also emphasised that evidence and outcome measurements remain inconsistent.

IPL/photofacial technology is listed in the clinic’s verified inventory. Eye-area suitability and protective measures must be confirmed before treatment.

Can Q-switched laser treat under-eye pigmentation?

Q-switched laser treatment may be considered for carefully selected pigment, but it is not automatically suitable for every dark-circle pattern.

The result depends on:

Pigment depth

Skin tone

Device wavelength

Energy settings

Presence of eczema

Post-inflammatory pigmentation risk

Structural shadowing

Recent reviews show variable results across laser types, and some wavelengths perform better than others for periocular pigmentation. A Q-switched treatment should therefore not be offered merely because the area looks dark.

Q-switched Nd:YAG technology is available at both clinic branches, subject to medical suitability.

Can fractional CO₂ laser improve the under-eye area?

Fractional CO₂ laser may be considered for selected patients with:

Fine lines

Crepey texture

Skin laxity

Photoageing

Certain textural components

It is not primarily a filler for tear-trough hollowing and may not be the first choice for pure pigment. A review of light devices reported that resurfacing lasers may help periocular wrinkles and skin density, but treatment close to the eye requires specialist precautions and eye protection.

Fractional CO₂ laser is available at both Aastha branches, but under-eye treatment must be confirmed after assessment.

Can dermal filler treat dark circles?

Dermal filler may reduce darkness caused mainly by a tear-trough hollow or structural shadow.

It does not directly remove:

Brown skin pigmentation

Eyelid eczema

Visible superficial veins

Significant eye bags

Fluid-related puffiness

Skin laxity

A proper examination must determine whether adding volume will improve the shadow or make puffiness and contour irregularity worse.

What are the risks of under-eye filler?

Possible complications include:

Bruising

Swelling

Puffiness

Asymmetry

Lumps

Bluish Tyndall effect

Infection

Delayed inflammatory reactions

Filler migration

Vascular occlusion

Skin injury

Rare permanent vision loss

Filler-related blindness is rare but potentially devastating because facial blood vessels communicate with the eye’s circulation.

Dermal fillers are available at both clinic locations, but tear-trough filler should be performed only after careful anatomical assessment, informed consent and confirmation that the patient is an appropriate candidate.

Who may not be suitable for tear-trough filler?

Filler may be unsuitable or require additional caution when there is:

Significant under-eye puffiness

Prominent eye bags

Poor lymphatic drainage

Very thin transparent skin

Active eyelid dermatitis

Skin infection

Previous filler complications

Unrealistic expectations

Certain anatomical risk factors

A better surgical indication

Some patients are better served by skincare, pigment treatment, surgical assessment or no procedure.

Can filler be dissolved?

Hyaluronic-acid filler can often be treated with hyaluronidase when medically required.

Dissolving treatment may be considered for:

Overcorrection

Persistent swelling

Lumps

Tyndall effect

Migration

Suspected vascular compromise

Dissolving does not guarantee immediate restoration of the original appearance, and an acute vascular event requires emergency management rather than a routine follow-up appointment.

Can PRP treat under-eye dark circles?

PRP has been studied for periocular rejuvenation, texture and pigmentation, but the available evidence remains limited and treatment protocols vary.

It should not be described as a guaranteed treatment for every dark-circle type. It is unlikely to correct a major tear-trough hollow or large eye bags by itself.

The clinic’s verified matrix confirms PRP/GFC for hair treatment, not specifically as an under-eye service. The website should not advertise under-eye PRP until its protocol and current availability are separately confirmed.

When is surgery considered?

An oculoplastic or plastic-surgical opinion may be appropriate when dark circles are mainly caused by:

Prominent lower-eyelid bags

Significant skin laxity

Excess lower-eyelid skin

Structural tissue descent

A deformity unlikely to respond to topical or minimally invasive treatment

Systematic reviews indicate that blepharoplasty is more appropriate than pigment treatment when excessive skin laxity is the main cause.

Aastha Skin Centre can assess the likely cause and advise whether external surgical referral is more appropriate.

Can concealer help?

Yes.

Colour correction and concealer can provide immediate camouflage without medical risk.

Depending on the under-eye tone:

Peach or orange corrector may reduce blue-grey appearance

A matching concealer can reduce contrast

Hydrating preparation can reduce product settling

Heavy layers may emphasise fine lines

Do cucumber, potato or cold spoons cure dark circles?

No home remedy permanently corrects pigment, vascular visibility or structural hollowing.

A cool compress may temporarily reduce mild puffiness, but lemon, baking soda, toothpaste and concentrated essential oils can cause:

Irritation

Eyelid dermatitis

Chemical burns

Watering

Post-inflammatory pigmentation

Accidental eye injury

Products not designed for use near the eyes should not be applied to the eyelids.

Does drinking more water remove dark circles?

Adequate hydration supports general health and may improve temporary dryness, but it does not remove hereditary pigment, visible blood vessels or a tear-trough deformity.

Dark circles should not be blamed solely on dehydration when they remain despite normal fluid intake.

The timeframe depends on the cause and treatment.

Skincare and pigment treatment usually work gradually

Dark circles alone are not an emergency.

Seek urgent ophthalmic or hospital care for:

Sudden vision loss or blurring

Eye pain

Double vision

One-sided rapidly increasing swelling

Fever with eyelid swelling

A protruding eye

Restricted eye movement

Severe redness

Sudden symptoms after filler injection

Pain, reduced vision, proptosis and impaired eye movement can indicate a serious orbital condition requiring immediate assessment.

Frequently asked questions

What causes dark circles under the eyes?

Dark circles may result from pigmentation, visible blood vessels, thin skin, eczema, eye rubbing, puffiness, tear-trough hollowing, ageing or a combination of factors.

Are dark circles caused only by lack of sleep?

No. Poor sleep can make them more noticeable, but hereditary pigmentation, facial anatomy, thin skin and allergies are common contributors.

Can creams permanently remove dark circles?

Creams may lighten pigment or improve fine texture, but they cannot fill a tear-trough hollow or remove prominent eye bags. Permanent complete removal cannot be guaranteed.

Can chemical peels or lasers treat dark circles?

They may improve selected pigmentary or textural dark circles. The procedure must be chosen according to skin tone, pigment depth, eye-area safety and the presence of structural shadowing.

Does under-eye filler remove pigmentation?

No. Filler can soften a shadow caused by tear-trough hollowing, but it does not directly remove brown pigment, eczema or visible superficial veins.

Is under-eye filler safe?

It can be appropriate for carefully selected patients, but risks include swelling, lumps, migration, vascular occlusion, skin injury and rare permanent vision loss.

When do dark circles need urgent medical attention?

Urgent care is needed when under-eye change occurs with sudden vision problems, eye pain, painful eye movement, fever, rapidly increasing swelling, double vision or protrusion of the eye.
