What is eczema?

Eczema is an umbrella term for several conditions that cause inflamed, itchy and excessively dry skin.

Types of eczema include:

Atopic dermatitis

Contact dermatitis

Hand eczema

Dyshidrotic eczema

Nummular eczema

Seborrhoeic dermatitis

Stasis dermatitis

Neurodermatitis

The correct treatment depends on the eczema type, affected body area, age, severity and whether infection or allergy is contributing.

What is atopic dermatitis?

Atopic dermatitis is a chronic inflammatory skin condition associated with:

Skin-barrier weakness

Immune-system activity

Genetic susceptibility

Environmental triggers

Excessively dry and sensitive skin

It often begins in infancy or childhood but can continue into adulthood or begin for the first time later in life.

Is eczema contagious?

No. Atopic eczema is not contagious.

It cannot spread through:

Touching

Sharing towels

Clothing

Swimming

Food

Kissing

Sexual contact

Living in the same household

An infection occurring on top of eczema may occasionally be contagious, but eczema itself does not pass between people.

What does eczema look like?

Eczema may cause:

Intense itching

Dry or rough skin

Inflamed patches

Scaling

Cracking

Oozing

Crusting

Small bumps

Thickened, leathery skin

Darker or lighter marks after a flare

Repeated scratching can thicken the skin and create an itch–scratch cycle in which itching causes scratching and scratching causes further inflammation.

How does eczema appear on Indian skin?

On lighter skin, active eczema may appear pink or red.

On medium-to-deep Indian skin tones, it may appear:

Dark brown

Purple

Grey

Dusky

Darker than surrounding skin

Less visibly red

Covered with small bumps

Thickened after repeated scratching

Temporary dark or light marks may remain after the active rash settles. Eczema should not be dismissed merely because bright redness is absent.

What causes atopic dermatitis?

Atopic dermatitis does not have one single cause.

It appears to result from an interaction between:

A weakened skin barrier

Immune inflammation

Genetic tendency

Environmental exposure

Skin microorganisms

Individual triggers

The weakened barrier allows water to escape and irritants to enter more easily, leaving the skin dry, sensitive and prone to inflammation.

What can trigger an eczema flare?

Possible triggers include:

Dry weather

Heat and sweating

Fragranced products

Harsh cleansers

Wool or rough fabrics

Dust

Stress

Skin infection

Detergents and cleaning products

Occupational chemicals

Contact allergy

Triggers differ between patients. A useful treatment plan identifies repeatable personal triggers without forcing the patient to avoid every item on a generic list.

Is eczema an allergy?

Not always.

Atopic dermatitis is associated with allergic conditions such as asthma, allergic rhinitis and food allergy, but eczema itself is not simply an allergic reaction to one food or product.

Contact dermatitis can be allergic when the immune system reacts to a substance touching the skin. Irritant contact dermatitis develops when repeated exposure directly damages the skin barrier.

Is eczema the same as contact dermatitis?

No, although both belong to the eczema group.

Atopic dermatitis

Often begins early in life

Is associated with skin-barrier weakness

May affect several body areas

Can flare without one identifiable external contact

Contact dermatitis

Develops where a substance touches the skin

May be irritant or allergic

Commonly affects the hands, face, neck, scalp or feet

Improves when the responsible exposure is identified and avoided

A patient can have atopic dermatitis and contact allergy at the same time.

Is eczema the same as fungal infection?

No.

Eczema

Is inflammatory

Is not contagious

Often causes widespread dryness and itching

May improve with appropriate anti-inflammatory treatment

Fungal infection

Is caused by fungi

May spread between people or body areas

Often has a scaly or expanding edge

Requires antifungal medicine

Steroid-containing mixed creams can temporarily hide fungal infection while allowing it to spread. Examination or fungal testing may be needed when the diagnosis is uncertain.

Can eczema look like psoriasis or scabies?

Yes.

Psoriasis may cause thicker, more sharply defined plaques and can affect the scalp, nails and joints. Scabies commonly causes severe night-time itching, burrows and similar symptoms in household contacts.

Nummular eczema can produce circular patches that resemble ringworm, while hand eczema may resemble fungal infection or psoriasis. Accurate diagnosis is important before continuing long-term steroid, antifungal or scabies treatment.

What is dyshidrotic eczema?

Dyshidrotic eczema, also called pompholyx, produces small, intensely itchy blisters on the:

Fingers

Palms

Sides of the hands

Toes

Soles

After the blisters settle, the skin may peel, crack or become painful.

Possible associations include sweating, stress, irritants, metal allergy and infection elsewhere on the hands or feet. Patch testing may be considered when contact allergy is suspected.

What is nummular eczema?

Nummular eczema produces round or oval itchy patches that may resemble coins.

The patches can become:

Dry

Scaly

Raised

Crusted

Oozing

Darker after healing

Because ringworm, psoriasis and bacterial infection can look similar, the diagnosis may require examination or testing before treatment.

How does Dr. Cheena Langer diagnose eczema?

A consultation may include:

Reviewing when the rash began

Examining its distribution and appearance

Assessing itching, pain and sleep disturbance

Reviewing soaps, skincare and hair products

Asking about work and household exposures

Checking for asthma, allergies or family history

Reviewing creams and tablets already used

Looking for bacterial, viral or fungal infection

Checking whether contact allergy is possible

Considering tests when the diagnosis remains uncertain

Atopic dermatitis is usually diagnosed through clinical examination and history rather than one specific blood test.

Are tests always required?

No. Many patients can be diagnosed without testing.

Tests may be considered when:

Fungal infection is possible

The rash repeatedly becomes infected

Contact allergy is suspected

Treatment is not working

Hair, nails or scalp are involved

The diagnosis is uncertain

An autoimmune or blistering disorder is possible

Systemic treatment is being planned

Possible investigations include:

Skin scraping

Bacterial or viral swab

Patch testing

Blood tests

Skin biopsy

What is patch testing?

Patch testing helps identify delayed allergic reactions to substances that contact the skin.

Small amounts of selected allergens are placed on the back beneath adhesive chambers. The test sites are examined later for a reaction.

Patch testing may be considered for:

Persistent hand eczema

Eyelid dermatitis

Facial dermatitis

Foot eczema

Occupational dermatitis

Reactions to cosmetics, jewellery or hair dye

Eczema that worsens despite treatment

Patch testing is different from skin-prick testing used for immediate allergies.

Availability and the required allergen series should be confirmed during consultation; it should not be assumed that every suspected allergy requires testing.

Can eczema be cured permanently?

Atopic dermatitis currently has no permanent cure, but it can often be controlled.

Treatment may help:

Reduce itching

Heal cracks and inflammation

Improve sleep

Prevent infection

Reduce flare frequency

Extend periods of clear or nearly clear skin

Reduce skin thickening

Long-term maintenance is often needed because symptoms may return after treatment stops.

What are the goals of eczema treatment?

Treatment aims to:

Restore the skin barrier

Reduce inflammation

Control itching

Heal damaged skin

Treat infection when present

Avoid individual triggers

Prevent repeated flares

Improve sleep and quality of life

The treatment plan depends on the location, severity, age and response to previous therapy.

Why is moisturiser important?

Moisturisers help reduce water loss and support the damaged skin barrier.

Regular use can:

Reduce dryness

Relieve discomfort

Prevent cracking

Reduce inflammation

Increase time between flare-ups

Improve tolerance of other treatments

AAD guidelines strongly recommend moisturisers as a central part of atopic dermatitis care.

Which moisturiser is suitable for eczema?

A suitable product is usually:

Fragrance-free

Thick enough for the level of dryness

Easy for the patient to apply regularly

Free from known allergens

Tolerated without burning

Ointments

Often useful for very dry, cracked skin because they reduce water loss effectively.

Creams

May be more comfortable during the day or in warm weather.

Lotions

Are lighter but may be insufficient for severely dry eczema.

The best moisturiser is one that controls dryness and can be used consistently.

When should moisturiser be applied?

Moisturiser may be applied:

Soon after bathing

Whenever the skin feels dry

After handwashing

Before exposure to cold weather

Regularly between flares

In addition to prescribed medicine

Moisturising should usually continue even when the visible rash has improved because the skin barrier may remain vulnerable.

How should someone with eczema bathe?

A simple bathing routine may include:

Use lukewarm rather than hot water

Keep bathing reasonably brief

Use a mild fragrance-free cleanser

Clean only where needed

Avoid aggressive scrubbing

Pat rather than rub dry

Apply moisturiser promptly afterwards

Bathing followed by moisturising can hydrate eczema-prone skin. The correct frequency depends on the patient, climate and disease pattern.

Which soaps and skincare products should be avoided?

Potential irritants include:

Fragranced soap

Strong body wash

Antiseptic liquid used routinely

Scrubs

Loofahs

Harsh exfoliating acids

Essential oils

Perfumed moisturisers

Alcohol-based products

Unprescribed fairness creams

Repeated chemical peels during active eczema

A product labelled “natural,” “organic” or “herbal” can still cause irritation or allergy.

How are topical corticosteroids used?

Topical corticosteroids reduce eczema inflammation, itching and swelling.

They are available in different strengths. Selection depends on:

Age

Body area

Severity

Skin thickness

Duration

Previous response

Whether infection is present

They are commonly used as first-line treatment for active eczema flares.

Are steroid creams safe?

Topical corticosteroids can be used safely when the correct strength, amount and duration are prescribed.

Possible problems from unsuitable or prolonged use include:

Skin thinning

Stretch marks

Visible blood vessels

Acne-like eruptions

Colour changes

Steroid rosacea

Masking of infection

The face, eyelids, groin and skin folds usually require lower-strength or alternative treatment compared with thick eczema on the palms or soles.

How much steroid cream should be applied?

The amount depends on the body area and preparation.

A dermatologist may explain dosing using the fingertip unit, which is the amount squeezed from a standard tube along an adult fingertip.

Using too little may fail to control inflammation, while prolonged overuse can increase side-effect risk. Follow the written plan rather than applying a thick layer repeatedly whenever itching occurs.

What are topical calcineurin inhibitors?

Tacrolimus and pimecrolimus are non-steroid anti-inflammatory medicines.

They may be considered for:

Face

Eyelids

Neck

Skin folds

Areas requiring frequent treatment

Patients in whom topical steroids are unsuitable

Temporary warmth, stinging or burning may occur when treatment begins. Current guidelines support these medicines for reducing inflammation, itching and flare frequency.

Are newer non-steroid eczema medicines available?

Newer topical treatment classes include:

PDE-4 inhibitors

Topical JAK inhibitors

AhR agonists

Current AAD guidelines include several of these options for selected patients. Exact suitability depends on age, body area, disease severity, pregnancy status, availability and current approval in India.

A website should not imply that every internationally approved medicine is automatically available or appropriate locally.

What is wet-wrap therapy?

Wet-wrap therapy involves:

Applying moisturiser or prescribed medicine

Covering the area with a damp layer

Adding a dry layer over it

Leaving it in place for the advised period

It can hydrate the skin, reduce scratching and increase absorption of treatment during selected severe flares.

Wet wraps used with topical steroids should begin under professional guidance, particularly for children or large body areas.

Are antibiotics needed for eczema?

Not routinely.

Antibiotics treat bacterial infection; they do not treat the underlying eczema inflammation. They may be prescribed when there are clear signs of bacterial infection such as pus, painful swelling, spreading redness or honey-coloured crusts.

Unnecessary antibiotic creams or tablets can cause allergy, side effects and antimicrobial resistance.

Do antihistamines treat eczema?

Antihistamines do not directly repair the skin barrier or control the main inflammation causing atopic dermatitis.

A doctor may occasionally consider an antihistamine for a particular associated allergy or short-term sleep difficulty, but it should not replace moisturiser and anti-inflammatory treatment. Routine antihistamine use is not recommended as the main treatment for childhood eczema.

Topical antihistamine creams may irritate skin and are generally avoided.

When are phototherapy or systemic medicines considered?

These treatments may be considered when eczema:

Is moderate or severe

Covers a large body area

Seriously affects sleep or daily life

Has not responded to an appropriate topical plan

Requires frequent strong topical treatment

Cannot be managed safely with creams alone

Options may include:

Medically supervised phototherapy

Biologic injections

Oral JAK inhibitors

Selected conventional immune-modifying medicines

AAD guidelines recommend several biologic and targeted systemic medicines for selected moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis patients and recommend against routine systemic corticosteroid use.

Aastha Skin Centre’s verified technology inventory does not currently list an eczema phototherapy unit. Patients requiring phototherapy may need coordinated referral to an appropriate centre.

Why are steroid tablets not used routinely?

Oral or injected corticosteroids may temporarily suppress severe eczema but can cause rebound worsening after treatment stops.

Repeated or prolonged use may also cause:

Raised blood sugar

Blood-pressure changes

Weight gain

Infection risk

Mood or sleep disturbance

Bone loss

Hormonal suppression

Current dermatology guidelines recommend against systemic corticosteroids as routine long-term atopic dermatitis treatment.

Does eczema require allergy testing?

Not every patient needs allergy testing.

Testing may be considered when:

A product repeatedly causes a local rash

Hand, eyelid or facial eczema persists

Occupational contact allergy is possible

A food causes an immediate reproducible reaction

Eczema remains uncontrolled despite following treatment

The diagnosis is uncertain

Broad allergy panels can detect sensitisation that may not be clinically responsible for the eczema. Testing should be guided by the history.

Should foods be removed from the diet?

Routine elimination of milk, wheat, egg, nuts or several other foods is not recommended merely because someone has eczema.

Unnecessary restriction can lead to:

Poor nutrition

Weight loss

Protein deficiency

Vitamin or mineral deficiency

Anxiety around food

Reduced growth in children

A targeted food-allergy assessment may be appropriate when a particular food causes an immediate, reproducible reaction. Diet should not replace skincare and medical treatment.

Can eczema become infected?

Yes. Dry, cracked and scratched skin allows bacteria and viruses to enter more easily.

Signs of possible infection include:

Honey-coloured crusts

Pus-filled bumps

Oozing

Increasing pain

Swelling

Warmth

Rapid worsening

Fever

Feeling unwell

Infected eczema may require antimicrobial treatment in addition to continuing an appropriate eczema-control plan.

What is eczema herpeticum?

Eczema herpeticum is a potentially serious herpes simplex infection affecting eczema-prone skin.

Warning signs include:

Rapidly appearing painful blisters

Punched-out sores

Fever

Fatigue

Swollen lymph nodes

Sudden widespread worsening

Eye-area involvement

It requires urgent medical assessment and antiviral treatment. It can become life-threatening in babies, children and immunocompromised patients.

Frequently asked questions

Is eczema contagious?

No. Atopic eczema is an inflammatory skin condition and cannot spread through touching, towels, clothing, food or close contact.

Can eczema be cured permanently?

Atopic dermatitis currently has no permanent cure, but moisturisers, anti-inflammatory treatment and trigger management can control symptoms and reduce flare-ups.

Is eczema the same as a fungal infection?

No. Eczema is inflammatory, while fungal infection is caused by fungi and requires antifungal treatment. The two may look similar, so examination or testing may be needed.

Are steroid creams safe for eczema?

Yes, when the correct strength, amount, body area and duration are prescribed. Unsupervised strong or prolonged use can cause skin thinning and other side effects.

Which moisturiser is suitable for eczema?

A fragrance-free cream or ointment that controls dryness and does not sting is usually suitable. Very dry skin often benefits from a thicker ointment.

Does eczema require allergy testing?

Not always. Patch or food-allergy testing is usually considered only when the history suggests contact allergy, an immediate food reaction or another specific cause.

When is eczema an emergency?

Painful widespread blisters, fever, rapidly worsening skin, pus, honey-coloured crusts or suspected eczema herpeticum require urgent medical assessment.
