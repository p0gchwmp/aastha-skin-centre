What is a hypertrophic scar?

A hypertrophic scar is a thick, raised scar that remains confined to the area of the original injury.

It may appear:

Red, pink, purple, brown or darker than nearby skin

Raised and firm

Itchy

Tender

Tight

Sensitive to pressure

Less flexible than surrounding skin

Hypertrophic scars may gradually flatten and soften over time, although improvement can take many months.

What is a keloid?

A keloid is an overgrown scar that extends beyond the boundaries of the original wound.

It may continue growing after the skin has healed and can become substantially larger than the injury that triggered it. Keloids may develop weeks, months or even years after a cut, burn, acne lesion, operation or piercing.

What is the difference between a keloid and a hypertrophic scar?

Hypertrophic scar

Stays within the original wound

Commonly appears soon after injury

May gradually flatten

Can remain red, thick or itchy

Often responds more predictably to treatment

Keloid

Extends beyond the original wound

May continue growing for months or years

Is less likely to flatten naturally

Has a stronger tendency to recur

Often requires combination treatment

The distinction affects treatment choice, expected response and recurrence counselling.

Can a hypertrophic scar turn into a keloid?

No.

They are related forms of excessive scarring but behave differently. A hypertrophic scar does not later “convert” into a keloid. A scar that continues spreading beyond the original wound may have been a keloid from the beginning or may need reassessment.

What causes raised scars?

Raised scars develop when wound healing produces excess collagen and disorganised scar tissue within the deeper skin.

Potential triggers include:

Surgery

Burns

Cuts

Acne

Chickenpox

Vaccinations

Ear piercing

Tattoos

Insect bites

Skin infections

Caesarean-section wounds

Trauma

Minor scratches in susceptible people

Keloids can occasionally follow surprisingly small injuries.

Areas of piercing

Keloids can occur on almost any injured area, but the palms, soles and eyelids are less commonly affected.

What symptoms can a keloid cause?

A keloid may cause:

Itching

Burning

Tenderness

Pain

Tightness

Sensitivity to clothing

Reduced movement when close to a joint

Emotional distress

Difficulty wearing earrings

Irritation from shaving

Symptoms can continue while the keloid is active and growing.

Are keloids dangerous?

Keloids are benign and do not spread through the body like cancer.

They can still cause significant problems when they:

Become painful

Itch continuously

Restrict joint movement

Interfere with clothing

Repeatedly become irritated

Grow after every attempted removal

Cause distress about appearance

A rapidly changing, ulcerated or bleeding lesion should be checked because another condition may resemble a raised scar.

Are keloids contagious?

No.

Keloids cannot spread through:

Touching

Towels

Clothing

Kissing

Swimming

Sexual contact

Sharing a bed

Living in the same household

They are an abnormal wound-healing response, not a bacterial, fungal or viral infection.

Who is more likely to develop keloids?

Risk is higher in people who:

Have previously developed a keloid

Have relatives with keloids

Have South Asian, African, Caribbean, Chinese or other melanin-rich ancestry

Are between approximately 10 and 30 years of age

Develop acne or injuries on high-risk body sites

Undergo piercing or elective skin procedures

Experience prolonged wound inflammation

Keloids can occur in any skin tone, but they are more frequent and often more noticeable in brown and black skin.

How do keloids appear on Indian skin?

On Indian skin, a keloid may appear:

Skin-coloured

Pink-brown

Red-brown

Purple

Dark brown

Shinier than nearby skin

Surrounded by pigmentation

Treatment can also produce dark or pale marks. Corticosteroid injections may occasionally cause lighter skin, while cryotherapy carries a meaningful risk of lasting pale pigmentation in darker skin tones.

Do keloids run in families?

They can.

A family history suggests inherited susceptibility to excessive scar formation. Patients should tell surgeons, dentists, dermatologists and piercing professionals when they or a close relative have developed keloids.

This information can influence wound closure, scar prevention and whether an elective procedure should be avoided.

Can acne cause keloids?

Yes.

Deep inflammatory acne can lead to:

Hypertrophic scars

Papular raised scars

Keloids

Dark post-inflammatory marks

Depressed scars occurring alongside raised scars

Raised acne scars require a different approach from indented acne scars. Early treatment of active acne can reduce the likelihood of additional scarring.

Can ear piercing cause keloids?

Yes.

Earlobe and cartilage piercings are common triggers in susceptible patients. Early signs may include:

Thickening around the piercing

Increasing firmness

Itching

A bump that continues enlarging

Extension beyond the original piercing hole

Early assessment may allow pressure treatment or injections to be considered before the lesion becomes large.

Should someone with a keloid get another piercing or tattoo?

Usually, extra caution is advisable.

A person who has already formed a keloid has a higher risk of developing another after:

Piercing

Tattooing

Cosmetic surgery

Elective skin procedures

Unnecessary mole removal

Permanent makeup

Avoiding non-essential skin injury is the most reliable form of prevention for strongly keloid-prone patients.

Can a caesarean-section scar become raised?

Yes.

A C-section scar may become hypertrophic or, less commonly, develop keloidal overgrowth.

Assessment considers:

Whether the scar extends beyond the incision

Pain or itching

Width and thickness

Previous infection

Tension across the wound

Pregnancy or breastfeeding status

Future pregnancy plans

Treatment may include silicone, injections or another scar-directed approach after the wound has completely healed.

Can burns cause hypertrophic scars?

Yes.

Burn injuries are a common cause of hypertrophic scarring, particularly when healing is prolonged or the wound extends deeply into the skin.

Burn scars can also cause:

Tightness

Reduced movement

Contractures

Itching

Pain

Pigment changes

Scars limiting a joint or causing a contracture may require coordinated dermatology, plastic-surgery, physiotherapy or burn-specialist care.

Can keloids develop without an obvious injury?

Occasionally, the triggering injury may have been so minor that the patient does not remember it.

Possible unnoticed triggers include:

Small acne lesions

Insect bites

Folliculitis

Minor scratches

Shaving injury

A small vaccination site

A previously inflamed hair follicle

A lesion with no convincing injury history may require closer examination or biopsy to confirm that it is truly a keloid.

Possible alternatives include:

Do keloids disappear naturally?

Keloids usually do not disappear without treatment.

They may eventually stop growing and become less symptomatic, but the raised tissue generally remains. Hypertrophic scars have a greater tendency to flatten and soften over time.

Can keloids be permanently cured?

No treatment can guarantee permanent cure.

Treatment may:

Flatten the scar

Soften it

Reduce redness or darkness

Relieve pain and itching

Improve movement

Make it less noticeable

Reduce further growth

Recurrence remains possible, especially after surgical removal. Combination treatment is often used because keloids respond unpredictably.

Does every raised scar need treatment?

No.

Observation may be reasonable when the scar:

Is small and stable

Causes no pain or itch

Does not restrict movement

Does not bother the patient

Has a clear benign diagnosis

Earlier treatment may be considered when a scar is actively thickening, painful, itchy or extending beyond the original wound.

Can silicone gel or sheets help?

Yes.

Medical silicone may help reduce:

Scar height

Firmness

Redness

Swelling

Itching

Stiffness

Silicone is applied only after the wound has closed; it should not be placed on an open wound or active scab. Daily use is often required for several months. Silicone sheets may work better than ointment for some patients, but they can occasionally cause irritation or skin breakdown.

Are silicone products useful for prevention?

They may help patients who are prone to raised scars.

Silicone gel or sheets can be started after the wound has healed and may reduce the risk of excessive thickening. Early use is often considered after:

Surgery

Burns

Injury

Keloid removal

Piercing complications

The product must be used consistently and should be stopped or reviewed if it causes a rash or wound breakdown.

Are corticosteroid injections used?

Intralesional corticosteroid injections are among the most established treatments for raised scars and keloids.

They may help:

Flatten the scar

Make it softer

Reduce itching

Reduce pain

Slow further growth

Several sessions are commonly needed. Possible adverse effects include skin thinning, small dents, visible blood vessels and lighter or darker pigmentation. Recurrence can occur after improvement.

Corticosteroid injections for keloids are not separately confirmed in the current clinic service matrix. Availability should be confirmed after consultation.

What are 5-FU or other scar injections?

A dermatologist may sometimes inject another medicine, such as 5-fluorouracil, alone or in combination with a corticosteroid.

These treatments may help reduce:

Scar thickness

Firmness

Pain

Itching

Recurrence risk when combined with other treatment

Possible effects include pain, swelling, irritation, ulceration or pigment change. Medicine selection depends on the scar and the patient’s medical history.

These injections are not separately verified as currently available at Aastha Skin Centre.

Can pressure earrings help earlobe keloids?

Yes.

A pressure earring may be used:

When early thickening develops after piercing

After keloid surgery

Alongside injections

To reduce recurrence

Pressure must be strong enough to influence the scar without damaging the skin. Devices often need to be worn for many hours daily over several months, which can be uncomfortable.

Is cryotherapy used?

Cryotherapy freezes scar tissue and may reduce the size, firmness, pain or itching of selected small keloids.

It may be combined with injections or another treatment. Possible effects include:

Pain

Blistering

Swelling

Crusting

Darkening

Permanent lightening

Recurrence

Permanent pale marks are an important concern in brown and black skin.

Cryotherapy is not separately confirmed in the clinic’s verified service matrix.

Can laser treatment improve a keloid?

Laser or light-based treatment may help reduce:

Redness

Scar height

Firmness

Itching

Pigment contrast

Laser is often combined with injections or pressure treatment rather than used alone. Different devices target redness, thickness or texture, so availability of a laser does not mean it is automatically suitable for every keloid.

Fractional CO₂ laser is available at both Aastha branches, but a keloid-specific laser plan must be confirmed after examination.

Is fractional CO₂ laser suitable for raised scars?

Fractional CO₂ laser may be used in selected scar-remodelling plans, particularly when texture, stiffness or treatment-assisted medicine delivery is being considered.

It is not automatically the first treatment for a large actively growing keloid. Aggressive treatment can create additional inflammation and, in a keloid-prone patient, potentially stimulate more scarring.

The diagnosis, scar activity and previous response must be reviewed before treatment.

Can MNRF treat keloids?

MNRF may have a role in selected scar-remodelling plans, but it should not be presented as a standard stand-alone cure for actively growing keloids.

Needling itself creates controlled skin injury. In a strongly keloid-prone patient, any procedure must be selected cautiously.

MNRF is confirmed at both branches, but its role in a specific raised scar should be decided only after examination.

Can surgery remove a keloid?

Surgery can physically remove a keloid, but surgery alone carries a very high risk of recurrence.

The new surgical wound can stimulate another keloid, which may grow as large as or larger than the original scar. Surgery is therefore commonly combined with measures such as injections, pressure therapy, silicone, cryotherapy or specialist radiation.

Keloid excision is not separately confirmed in the current clinic service matrix. Larger or complex scars may require referral to a dermatologic, plastic or reconstructive surgeon.

Is radiation therapy used after keloid surgery?

Specialist superficial radiation may be considered after surgical removal of selected recurrent or difficult keloids to reduce the chance of regrowth.

It requires:

Careful dose planning

Protection of surrounding tissue

Coordination with an oncology or radiation centre

Detailed discussion of benefits and long-term risks

Radiation is not routine for every keloid and is not listed as an in-clinic Aastha service. External specialist referral would be required when appropriate.

Treatment may make a raised scar:

Flatter

Softer

Less itchy

Less red or dark

More flexible

Less noticeable

Can treatment make a keloid worse?

It can.

A poorly selected or overly aggressive procedure may cause:

Additional inflammation

Ulceration

Infection

New pigmentation

A larger recurrent scar

New keloid formation at needle or surgical sites

This is why diagnosis, scar activity and previous healing history should be reviewed before cutting, burning or lasering the scar.

Can home remedies flatten a keloid?

No home remedy can reliably remove a keloid.

Avoid applying:

Garlic

Lemon

Vinegar

Toothpaste

Bleach

Strong acids

Unregulated steroid combinations

Needles

Blades

Tight clips that injure the skin

These approaches can cause burns, infection, ulceration, pigmentation and additional scarring.

Can massage remove a keloid?

Massage may improve comfort or flexibility in some healing scars, but it cannot reliably flatten a mature keloid.

Forceful massage of an inflamed, painful or recently healed scar can irritate the area. Scar massage should be started only when the wound has fully closed and the treating clinician considers it appropriate.

How can new keloids be prevented?

Risk reduction may include:

Avoiding unnecessary piercings and tattoos

Treating acne early

Informing surgeons about previous keloids

Keeping wounds clean and moist while healing

Reducing tension across surgical wounds

Using silicone after the wound closes

Seeking early review when a scar begins thickening

Using pressure treatment when recommended

Protecting scars from sunlight

People with a strong history should discuss prevention before elective procedures.

Why is sunscreen important?

Sun exposure can make a healing scar darker and increase contrast with nearby skin.

Once the wound has closed, patients may be advised to use:

Broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher

Protective clothing

Shade

Consistent reapplication during outdoor exposure

Sun protection does not flatten a keloid, but it can reduce avoidable pigment changes.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a keloid and a hypertrophic scar?

A hypertrophic scar remains inside the original wound, while a keloid grows beyond the wound’s boundaries and may continue enlarging.

Can a keloid disappear naturally?

Keloids usually do not disappear naturally. They may eventually stop growing, but the raised tissue generally remains unless treatment reduces it.

Can keloids be permanently removed?

Permanent removal cannot be guaranteed. Keloids can recur after injections, laser or surgery, and surgery alone has a particularly high recurrence risk.

Do steroid injections flatten keloids?

Corticosteroid injections can soften, flatten and relieve itching or pain in many raised scars, but multiple sessions may be needed and recurrence can occur.

Can silicone gel treat a keloid?

Silicone gel or sheets may reduce scar height, firmness, redness and itching when used consistently after the wound has closed.

Should a keloid be surgically removed?

Surgery may be considered for selected scars but is generally combined with additional treatment because removal alone frequently leads to recurrence.

Can piercing cause another keloid?

Yes. Someone who has previously formed a keloid has an increased risk after another piercing, tattoo or elective skin procedure.
